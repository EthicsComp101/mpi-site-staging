Ethical Principles

A guide to the principles students use to analyse ethics cases: the Principles of Biomedical Ethics, research ethics, AI ethics frameworks and more, with tips for using them in a competition answer.

Ethical cases often put good reasons on both sides. A treatment may save someone’s life while violating their wishes. An AI system may help thousands of people while unfairly excluding a few. Principles help us explain why those concerns matter and decide what to do.

A principle is a broad guide to action, such as respecting people’s choices. A theory offers a wider account of what makes actions right. A rule gives more specific guidance. Principles connect these levels: they help turn ethical ideas into questions about a particular decision.

The examples below are invented for practice. Use them to test arguments, then ask which details would change your conclusion.

1. Principles of biomedical ethics

Tom Beauchamp and James Childress developed an influential approach built around four principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. Principlism is the name commonly given to this approach.

These principles create genuine obligations, but none automatically wins every conflict. Philosophers call such obligations prima facie: binding unless a stronger obligation outweighs them in the circumstances. The task is to explain what each principle requires here and justify any trade-off. Varkey’s overview introduces the approach through clinical cases.

Respect for autonomy: support informed choices

Meaning: People should have a meaningful say over decisions about their own bodies and lives.

What it asks: Give understandable information, check understanding, avoid pressure and take the person’s values seriously. Disagreement with a doctor does not by itself show that a patient cannot decide. Supporting choice may require an interpreter, more time or help communicating.

Example: An adult understands that a proposed operation could extend their life but declines because recovery would prevent them spending their remaining months at home. Respect for autonomy gives a strong reason to honour that refusal.

Tensions: Beneficence supports offering treatment that could help; non-maleficence draws attention to the harm of leaving illness untreated. Justice limits demands on shared resources. A right to refuse treatment does not create an unlimited claim to any treatment someone requests.

Beneficence: actively help

Meaning: Promote people’s welfare and protect their important interests.

What it asks: Identify the benefit, who receives it and how likely it is. Consider the person’s own account of a good outcome. Longer life, less pain, independence and time with family can matter differently to different patients.

Example: A clinic offers home visits to patients who repeatedly miss appointments because they cannot travel. Helping requires changing the service rather than simply sending more reminders.

Tensions: Helping can become paternalism, overriding someone’s choice for their supposed benefit. A beneficial intervention may also cause serious harm or consume resources others need. Good intentions are insufficient: ask whether the proposed help works. Beauchamp’s discussion of beneficence explores these limits.

Non-maleficence: avoid causing harm

Meaning: We have a duty to avoid inflicting harm. The term literally concerns not doing evil or harm.

What it asks: Identify physical, psychological and other serious harms; compare their likelihood and severity; and seek safer alternatives. Avoiding every risk is impossible. The important question is whether exposing someone to this risk is justified.

Example: A hospital considers a new test that sometimes detects disease earlier but frequently triggers painful, unnecessary follow-up procedures. Earlier detection alone does not settle whether to introduce it.

Tensions: Beneficence may support accepting some harm to achieve a larger benefit. Autonomy may support a patient’s informed acceptance of risk. Justice asks whether one group bears the dangers while another gets the gains. Also compare action with inaction: declining to intervene can leave preventable harm unaddressed.

Justice: distribute benefits and burdens fairly

Meaning: Treat people fairly and give proper weight to their claims, including when resources are scarce.

What it asks: Explain which differences are relevant. Equal treatment, priority to greater need and achieving the greatest benefit can point in different directions. Fairness also concerns the process: consistent rules, reasons people can understand and a way to challenge mistakes.

Example: A hospital has one remaining rehabilitation place. One patient would gain more mobility; another has greater existing disadvantage. Choosing fairly requires defending the allocation rule, rather than declaring either person more deserving overall.

Tensions: Helping the person in front of you may reduce help available to others. Individual preferences can conflict with fair access. Preventing the most harm overall can still leave particular groups unfairly burdened. Ask whose needs the decision-maker may be overlooking.

Informed consent, confidentiality and truth-telling

Informed consent is a process of understanding and voluntary agreement. A signature alone cannot establish it. The person needs relevant information about the intervention, expected benefits, risks and alternatives, including doing nothing. They must be able to decide about this choice and communicate agreement without coercion.

The American Medical Association’s consent guidance also addresses urgent treatment when the patient cannot participate and no surrogate is available. A surrogate is someone authorised to decide for another person. Such exceptions need reasons; convenience is insufficient. Where children are involved, distinguish legal permission from their own understanding and willingness to participate.

Confidentiality means protecting information entrusted to you. It supports privacy, trust and honest disclosure. A patient might avoid seeking help if they fear their diagnosis will become public. However, serious threats to safety and some legal duties can justify limited disclosure. Ask who needs to know, what they need and whether a less intrusive response would work. AMA guidance stresses limiting disclosure to necessary information.

Truth-telling supports informed choices and trust. Explain uncertainty honestly rather than offering false reassurance. Being truthful does not require delivering distressing information without care. Ask what the person wants to know and how to communicate it sensitively. A family’s wish to conceal a diagnosis deserves attention, but it does not automatically override the patient’s preferences.

The doctrine of double effect

The doctrine of double effect distinguishes harm intended as an aim or means from harm foreseen as a side effect. On a common formulation, an action with a harmful side effect may be permissible when the act is otherwise acceptable, the harm is unintended, the good is not achieved through that harm, and there is a sufficiently serious reason to accept the risk.

Imagine rescuers opening a floodgate to protect a town while foreseeing damage to an empty storage building. That differs from destroying someone’s building as the means of forcing them to cooperate. Whether either action is justified still depends on alternatives and proportionality, meaning whether the harm is excessive relative to the good pursued.

Saying “I meant well” proves very little. Intentions require scrutiny, foreseeable harm still matters, and avoidable damage still needs justification. Critics question whether intention should make such a moral difference. The Stanford entry also cautions against assuming that appropriate pain relief normally shortens life.

Criticisms and alternative approaches

Too much flexibility: In their critique of principlism, K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert argue that the principles lack a unified theoretical structure for resolving conflicts. Two people can invoke the same list and reach opposite answers. Calling a decision “balanced” does not explain why its balance is fair.

A principlist response is to make broad duties more precise, then give reasons for their relative weight. Specification means saying exactly what a principle requires in this setting. Balancing means explaining why one competing duty should take priority. Beauchamp discusses this method in Methods and principles in biomedical ethics.

Too abstract: Casuistry, or reasoning through cases, compares the disputed case with clearer examples. What makes this situation like an accepted refusal of treatment? What important difference might justify another outcome? Jonsen’s account of casuistry explains this method. Its weakness is that accepted examples may carry inherited prejudice.

Too individualistic: Care ethics asks about dependence, relationships, power and the work of caring. Offering a choice may achieve little if someone lacks support to act on it. Who will provide care, and who bears that burden? The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy explains this approach. Care and principles can inform each other: attending to relationships can improve how autonomy and justice are understood.

2. Research ethics

Research seeks knowledge that may help future people. That aim can tempt researchers to treat present participants as tools. These frameworks explain why scientific value needs safeguards.

The Belmont Report

The US Belmont Report, published in 1979, emerged from the National Commission created by the 1974 National Research Act. It sought to guide human research after serious abuses had exposed the need for stronger protection.

Its three principles are:

Respect for persons: Recognise people’s ability to choose and protect those with reduced ability to decide freely.

Beneficence: Avoid harm and assess how to maximise possible benefits while minimising possible harms.

Justice: Select participants fairly and distribute research burdens and benefits fairly.

These principles inform consent, risk assessment and participant selection. Imagine recruiting only poorly paid workers for a risky study because they are easy to persuade. Consent would not, by itself, answer the concern about exploitation. Equally, excluding a group from research can be unfair if it leaves their health needs poorly understood.

The Nuremberg Code

The Nuremberg Code arose in 1947 from the judgment in the Doctors’ Trial after Nazi medical atrocities. Its ten points make voluntary consent central and require scientifically justified research, avoidance of unnecessary suffering, proportionate risks and qualified researchers. Participants must be free to end participation, and researchers must stop when continuing is likely to cause serious injury, disability or death.

The lesson is stark: neither authority nor a promise of useful knowledge erases obligations to the person undergoing the experiment. Ask whether refusal is genuinely possible. An invitation from a prison official or someone controlling access to essential services can carry pressure even without an explicit threat.

The Declaration of Helsinki

The World Medical Association adopted the Declaration of Helsinki in 1964 to guide medical research involving humans; its current revision dates from 2024. It develops protections suited to research involving patients, healthy volunteers and identifiable human material or data.

It addresses independent ethics review, informed consent, risk assessment, privacy, vulnerability, research registration and reporting results. The 2024 text also stresses meaningful engagement with participants and communities, fair distribution of burdens and benefits, and environmental sustainability.

For a competition case, ask two separate questions: is the study worth doing, and is this way of doing it acceptable? Independent review matters because researchers may overestimate benefits or overlook pressures on participants. Approval is a safeguard, while ethical responsibility continues throughout the study.

3. AI and technology ethics principles

AI frameworks often echo biomedical ethics: help people, prevent harm, preserve choice and treat people fairly. Floridi and Cowls explicitly adapt those four principles. The institutional frameworks below also draw on human rights, democratic values and environmental concerns. Their overlapping vocabulary does not mean they are identical.

OECD AI Principles

The OECD AI Principles, adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024, identify five values-based principles:

Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being: AI should benefit people and the planet.

Human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy: Protect rights throughout the system’s life.

Transparency and explainability: Give meaningful information about AI use, limits and outputs.

Robustness, security and safety: Systems should function reliably and allow safe intervention when things go wrong.

Accountability: People and organisations must answer for their roles in AI development and use.

For an automated hiring tool, ask whose applications it rejects, whether those applicants can challenge errors and who has authority to fix the system. A company’s average accuracy figure cannot answer every fairness concern.

UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI

UNESCO’s 2021 Recommendation centres human rights and dignity, inclusive societies, diversity and environmental well-being. Its ten principles cover proportionality and avoiding harm; safety and security; privacy; inclusive, adaptable governance; accountability; transparency and explainability; human oversight; sustainability; public awareness; and fairness.

This wider lens matters when an AI service seems useful to its immediate users. Consider a school chatbot: students may gain help, while data collection affects privacy and the service consumes energy and water. Ask whether affected people can shape the decision and whether benefits and costs fall on different communities. Acknowledging an environmental cost does not settle its weight; compare it with realistic alternatives.

EU guidelines for trustworthy AI

The European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group published its Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI in 2019. They describe trustworthy AI as lawful, ethical and robust, meaning dependable in both technical and social settings.

Seven requirements translate that idea into practice: human agency and oversight; technical robustness and safety; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non-discrimination and fairness; societal and environmental well-being; and accountability.

These are ethical guidelines, distinct from the EU AI Act. In an exam-monitoring case, meaningful oversight requires more than a staff member approving an automated flag. Ask whether that person understands the evidence, can reverse the outcome and has time to investigate. Students also need an accessible way to challenge mistakes.

Floridi and Cowls: five principles

In their 2019 framework, Luciano Floridi and Josh Cowls retain beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy and justice, then add explicability. This combines intelligibility, understanding how a system works, with accountability, identifying who is responsible.

Suppose AI helps allocate housing. We need to assess benefits, harms, choice and fairness, but also understand enough about decisions to challenge them and assign responsibility. An explanation alone cannot make discrimination acceptable. Nor does publishing complex source code ensure that affected people understand a decision.

For Global AI Debates, turn each principle into a testable question. What evidence would show benefit? Which errors matter most? Who can refuse or appeal? What happens when safeguards fail? International recommendations and scholarly frameworks offer reasons to consider; naming one does not establish that a proposal is ethical or legally required.

4. Other principle sets found in cases
The precautionary principle

The precautionary principle concerns action under uncertainty. Principle 15 of the Rio Declaration says that lack of full scientific certainty should not delay cost-effective measures against threats of serious or irreversible environmental damage. It supports taking credible dangers seriously before all evidence is available. It does not demand banning everything uncertain. For a proposed pesticide, compare possible ecosystem damage, the evidence, available safeguards and the harms of withholding it. A limited trial with monitoring may be preferable to either unrestricted release or an indefinite ban.

Mill’s harm principle

In On Liberty, John Stuart Mill argues that coercing people against their will is justified to prevent harm to others; protecting a person from their own choices is insufficient by itself. His argument focuses on people capable of mature self-government. For a speech restriction, identify the alleged harm, its likelihood and whether less restrictive responses would work. Discomfort, offence and disagreement do not automatically establish harm. Showing some harm also leaves further questions about whether a particular restriction is necessary and proportionate.

Just war principles

Jus ad bellum concerns when resorting to war can be justified: common requirements include just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, proportionality, last resort and a reasonable prospect of success. Jus in bello concerns conduct within war, including distinguishing civilians from military targets, necessity and proportionality in attacks. The Stanford discussion of war explains disputes within this tradition. A justified defensive aim does not authorise every tactic. In a drone-strike case, assess the decision to use force separately from the target and foreseeable civilian harm. These moral questions overlap with law without being identical to it.

Professional codes: engineering and journalism

Professional codes connect broad principles to responsibilities that come with a role. The NSPE engineering code prioritises public safety and requires competence, honesty and attention to conflicts of interest. An engineer assessing a dangerous bridge must consider duties beyond pleasing the client. The SPJ journalism code centres truth, minimising harm, independence and accountability. Publishing an identifiable victim’s story may serve public understanding while causing avoidable harm. A code helps identify duties, while its authority and interpretation still need examination.

Business and stakeholder principles

A stakeholder is someone whose interests a business affects, such as an employee, customer, supplier, investor or neighbour. Stakeholder approaches ask managers to consider responsibilities across these groups. Honesty, fair treatment, avoiding harm and keeping commitments provide practical starting points. Beauchamp’s beneficence entry discusses stakeholder responsibilities. For a factory closure, compare workers’ livelihoods, the firm’s survival and community dependence. Naming every stakeholder is only the start: explain which claims deserve priority and why. Consultation, notice and transition support may reduce harms even when closure remains justified.

5. How to use principles in a competition answer
Build an argument around the decision

Start with the precise choice: who must decide, between which realistic options, and who is affected? Separate known facts from assumptions. Then use this sequence:

Name the main tension. Select the two or three principles doing the most work. Explain how each supports a different course of action.

Apply them to the details. Identify the people, benefits, harms, choices or unfairness involved. Test whether the evidence supports your claims.

Weigh the reasons. Consider severity, likelihood, reversibility, distribution and alternatives. Explain why a particular duty has greater weight here.

Face the strongest objection. Present the best reason against your answer fairly, then explain your response or revise your proposal.

State a qualified conclusion. Give a clear recommendation, practical safeguards and any fact that would change your answer.

Avoid counting principles as votes. Three labels supporting a proposal do not necessarily outweigh one serious violation. Look for options that reduce the conflict, such as narrower access to information, independent review or a reversible trial.

A short model answer

Suppose a school proposes AI monitoring of every student’s private messages to detect bullying.

“Our main tension is preventing harm versus privacy and autonomy. Protecting students gives the school a strong reason to act. However, scanning every private conversation is highly intrusive, and the case gives no evidence that it outperforms narrower measures. We recommend confidential reporting, trained staff and targeted intervention where there is credible evidence of danger. The strongest objection is that hidden bullying may go unreported. That deserves serious weight, so the school should review whether these measures protect students effectively. We would reconsider broader monitoring only with strong evidence of necessity, strict limits and an effective appeal process.”

Use commentary to advance the discussion

In an Ethics Olympiad or Ethics Bowl commentary, identify what the presenting team understood well, then test one important step. Have they justified the harm they predict? Does their safeguard protect those most at risk? Would their reasoning apply consistently to a similar case?

In your response, answer the objection directly. Conceding a sound point can strengthen your position. Explain what it changes, what remains justified and why. Principles earn their place by helping everyone understand the decision.

6. Further reading

Principles of Clinical Ethics and Their Application to Practice, Basil Varkey (2021). Clear introduction to the four principles, truth-telling and clinical conflicts.

The Principle of Beneficence in Applied Ethics, Tom L. Beauchamp; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2019). Explains helping, paternalism and business stakeholder responsibilities.

Informed Consent, Opinion 2.1.1, American Medical Association (n.d.). Concise guidance on communication, consent and emergency exceptions.

Confidentiality, Opinion 3.2.1, American Medical Association (n.d.). Explains trust, disclosure exceptions and sharing only necessary information.

Doctrine of Double Effect, Alison McIntyre; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2023). Distinguishes intended harm from side effects and examines objections.

A critique of principlism, K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert (1990). Original criticism of conflicting principles without a unified theory (free abstract).

Casuistry as methodology in clinical ethics, Albert R. Jonsen (1991). Introduces comparison with clearer cases (free abstract).

Care Ethics, Maureen Sander-Staudt; Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (n.d.). Adds relationships, dependency, power and the work of caring.

The Belmont Report, US National Commission; HHS/OHRP (1979). Primary text on respect for persons, beneficence and justice in research.

The Nuremberg Code, US Holocaust Memorial Museum (1947). Provides all ten points and their historical context.

Declaration of Helsinki, World Medical Association (2024). Current principles for medical research, review and participant protection.

OECD AI Principles, OECD (2024). Connects AI benefits, rights, explainability, safety and accountability.

Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, UNESCO (2021). Lists values, ten principles and policy areas, including sustainability.

Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, European Commission High-Level Expert Group on AI (2019). Turns broad AI values into seven practical requirements.

A Unified Framework of Five Principles for AI in Society, Luciano Floridi and Josh Cowls (2019). Explains the biomedical connection and explicability.

Rio Declaration on Environment and Development, United Nations (1992). Principle 15 gives an influential formulation of environmental precaution.

On Liberty, John Stuart Mill (1859). Primary source for the harm principle, especially Chapters I and IV.

War, Seth Lazar; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2016). Explains and questions just war principles and their application.

NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers, National Society of Professional Engineers (2019). Role-based duties concerning public safety, competence and conflicts.

SPJ Code of Ethics, Society of Professional Journalists (2026). Journalism principles for truth, harm, independence and accountability.
