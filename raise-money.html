Raise Money for Your Team

Most student teams cover their costs with a mix of small grants, local sponsors and one or two simple fundraisers. Here's how other teams do it, and everything you need to do the same.

What you're raising money for
Entry fees and coaching: get in touch, tell us your goals and we'll work something out with you. If you can't cover the cost, you can apply for fee support while you fundraise.

1. Ask local businesses to sponsor you

This is usually the fastest route. Small local businesses like pizzerias, delis, dentists, realtors, law firms, banks and hardware stores often have a small budget for local youth activities, and they like being thanked publicly. Service clubs (Rotary, Lions, Kiwanis) also support student teams.

How other teams do it

Carmel High School Speech & Debate (Indiana) Four sponsor levels from $100 to $1,000+. Every sponsor gets a thank-you on the website and social media. Higher levels add a logo, a mention in the newsletter and their logo on team shirts. Sponsors include local law firms and a therapy practice, and they sign up through a simple online form.
Great Neck South High School robotics (Long Island, NY) Supporter levels from $100 to $2,500+. Local sponsors sit alongside big names: the Gold Coast–Lake Success Rotary Club, the local fire company, the PTSA, an alumni class, Centre Pizza, Rosa's Pizza, Otto's Deli and Ace Hardware.
Miller Place High School robotics (Long Island, NY) Local sponsors include a plumbing company, a Wendy's franchise and a local restaurant, all thanked on the team's website.
Team PLUSH (Ottawa, Canada) Sponsor levels from C$300, plus an "in-kind" level for businesses that donate services or supplies instead of money.
The Cathedral School Llandaff (Cardiff, UK) Students pitched local firms themselves, and a solicitors' firm, a pharmacy, a builder, an estate agent and two restaurants sponsored the team.

Tips that make the ask work

  • Students make the ask. Businesses respond much better to students than to adults. Practise first.
  • Ask for something specific: "Your sponsorship covers our team's entry to an international ethics competition."
  • Start warm. Try parents' employers, businesses your families use, and ones that already sponsor local sports or theatre. Expect most cold asks to say no; that's normal.
  • Keep the letter to one page, with the ask in the first paragraph. Use your school or club email, not a personal one.
  • Keep a simple list of who you asked and what they said, and follow up once.
  • Thank sponsors publicly and quickly: a tagged post, a handwritten note, and a photo of the team at the event. Then ask again next season.
  • Ask about employer matching. Many companies (e.g. Bank of America, Starbucks, IBM) match employees' gifts to public schools and PTAs.

Sponsorship letter template

Dear [Name], We're the [School] [Ethics Team name], a group of [number] students who compete in international ethics competitions run by Modus Ponens Institute and TKEthics. We analyse real moral questions, from AI to online privacy, and compete against schools across North America, Europe and Asia. We're looking for local sponsors for our [season/year]. A sponsorship of [amount] covers one team's entry to the [Event, date]. Every sponsor will be thanked on our [Instagram/website], and sponsors of [amount] or more will have their logo on our team shirts. Would [Business] be willing to support us? We'd be happy to come by and tell you more. Thank you, [Student names], [School] [Team name] [Club email] · Advisor: [Teacher name, school email]

Want it on letterhead with our details and a quote attached? Email us and we'll send one.

2. Run a simple fundraiser

  • Restaurant give-back night. A restaurant donates part of one evening's sales to your team, and you bring friends and family. Examples:
    • Chipotle: 25% of sales, with a $150 minimum.
    • Blaze Pizza: 20% of sales.
    • Panera: 20–25%, but only for 501(c)(3)s, so book it through your PTA or booster club.
    Local pizzerias often agree to do the same if you ask. Promote it for a couple of weeks beforehand and have students there on the night.
  • Bake sale at lunch, at a school event or at parents' evening. Get permission for the spot first.
  • Host a public ethics showcase night. Your team debates a big question in front of parents and the community, and you sell tickets or take donations at the door. It's great practice too.
  • Other ideas: a car wash, a movie or game night, a read-a-thon with pledges, or a group yard sale.
  • Raffles: these count as gambling in many places and often need a permit, so check local rules first.
  • Online donation page: best for one specific goal ("Send our team to the Finals"), shared once with family and friends. Use your school's or PTA's own giving page if they have one. Online fundraising on its own often falls short, so pair it with sponsors.

3. Do it properly

In the US

  • Get your advisor's OK first, and the principal's for anything public or online.
  • Money goes through the school, not a student's or teacher's personal account. Usually that's the school's club (extraclassroom) account, or a PTA or booster club.
  • Tax receipts: gifts to a public school district, or to a PTA or booster club with 501(c)(3) status, are generally tax-deductible for the donor. That helps when asking businesses.
  • Arizona: residents can give up to $200 ($400 for couples) to a public school's extracurricular activities and get it back dollar-for-dollar as a state tax credit. Ask your school's front office.
  • DonorsChoose: teachers at public schools can post projects there. Your advisor would set it up.
  • Photos and names: follow your school's rules before posting students' names or photos online.

In the UK

  • Run money through the school fund or your PTA/PTFA.
  • If the PTA is a registered charity, donations from UK taxpayers can get Gift Aid (an extra 25p per £1).
  • Local Rotary clubs often support school debating. Check the funding finder for UK grants too.

4. Your checklist

  1. Agree a goal with your advisor (e.g. enough for two team entries).
  2. Check where the money will go: the school club account or PTA.
  3. Set up a simple team page or Instagram post saying who you are, what you do and how sponsors are thanked.
  4. Make a list of 15–20 local businesses and people to ask, warm ones first.
  5. Send the letter, visit in person and follow up once.
  6. Pick one fundraiser (a give-back night or a bake sale).
  7. Look for a grant in the funding finder, and email us for a quote or letter of support.
  8. Thank everyone, share your results, and ask again next season.

This is general guidance, not legal or tax advice. Rules vary by school, district and country, so always check with your school first.
